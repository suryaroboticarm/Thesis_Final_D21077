\begin{abstract}
Continuous trajectory tracking requires differential inverse kinematics (IK) at
every control timestep; for non-spherical wrist manipulators, kinematic coupling
renders this a nonlinear problem. Numerical solvers that resolve waypoints independently \textcolor{black}{can produce joint-space discontinuities; seeding from the previous solution, joint velocity limiting, and trajectory smoothing reduce but do not remove this behavior.} We address this through a coupled reward function,
combining a min-operator inverse-quadratic pose reward, asymmetric progress
shaping, and an exponential \textcolor{black}{joint velocity penalty}, that enforces simultaneous position-orientation convergence while suppressing velocity discontinuities,
applied here to a Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) controller mapping a
19-dimensional observation directly to joint velocity commands. The policy is
trained in Isaac Lab on 1.5~M joint-limit-aware reachable workspace poses, with
no trajectory supervision. The proposed method achieves competitive position
and orientation accuracy in simulation against baselines spanning a numerical
IK solver, classical velocity-level controllers, and contemporary RL methods,
and outperforms the alternative RL baselines \textcolor{black}{(TRPO-D, trained on the identical reward, and SAC-S)}. Zero-shot sim-to-real transfer to the Kinova
Gen3 Lite at 20~Hz closed-loop frequency achieves a position RMSE of 6.3~mm
and orientation RMSE of 0.032~rad on circular trajectory tracking. The policy generalizes without retraining to square, lemniscate, and 3-D Lissajous trajectories, demonstrating that a
velocity-level differential IK mapping learned purely on static pose reaching
transfers directly to continuous trajectory tracking at deployment, \textcolor{black}{demonstrated on the four trajectory geometries evaluated here}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{intro}
Robotic manipulators are indispensable to modern industrial automation, serving as the backbone for tasks ranging from precision assembly to complex material handling. To meet the demands of rapid advancements in the field, contemporary systems must provide not only mechanical precision but also intelligent, computationally efficient manipulation in real-time \cite{intro_1}. Traditionally, spherical wrist designs have dominated the industry due to their kinematic decoupling, which facilitates independent, closed-form analytical solutions for position and orientation \cite{intro_2}. While these designs have long been the industrial standard, they impose significant structural limitations on modern applications \cite{shah2019comparison}. The geometric constraint requiring the final three axes to intersect at a common point,  often compromises mechanical stiffness and restricts the integration of high-torque motors within compact wrist assemblies \cite{ostermeier}. Consequently, non-spherical wrist manipulators have gained prominence. By introducing offsets between the last three axes as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:sphericalvsnonspherical}, these configurations achieve superior structural integrity and higher payload-to-weight ratios \cite{carbonari2023inverse}. However, this violation of kinematic decoupling renders analytical inverse kinematics (IK) intractable, necessitating a reliance on complex numerical or data-driven solvers.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{Fig_1.jpg}
    \caption{(a) KUKA KR-240 Manipulator with Spherical Wrist (b) Kinova Gen3 Lite Manipulator with Non-spherical Wrist}
    \label{fig:sphericalvsnonspherical}
\end{figure}

Modern industrial practices require far beyond standard pick and place operations \cite{intro_4}. Tasks such as continuous inspection and dynamic human-robot collaborative handoff increasingly require manipulators to track end-effector trajectories whose target poses are determined online and cannot be fully enumerated or precomputed in advance, in contrast to fixed, repeated motion profiles for which offline trajectory planning remains both sufficient and preferable \cite{11358968}. These operations require the manipulator to maintain a precise end-effector pose along a continuous Cartesian path, requiring joint velocity resolution at the hardware's closed-loop control frequency \cite{intro_6, intro_7}. However, for non-spherical wrist manipulators, this requirement introduces a fundamental kinematic bottleneck. The offset joint structure creates a unified, non-linear system where position and orientation are deeply coupled, requiring the inverse kinematics to be solved as a single optimization at every control timestep.

In this work, we present an end-to-end PPO-based differential inverse kinematics controller that directly maps instantaneous Cartesian pose errors to joint velocity commands, bypassing any dependence on numerical solvers or explicit Jacobian computation. A novel coupled reward function combining a min-operator pose reward, asymmetric progress shaping, and exponential \textcolor{black}{joint velocity penalty} enforces simultaneous position-orientation convergence while producing smooth, hardware-safe velocity commands. Sim-to-real transfer is validated on physical Kinova Gen3 Lite hardware at 20 Hz.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section~\ref{related work} surveys existing works on numerical IK solvers, velocity-level controllers, and learning-based approaches. Section~\ref{method} details the proposed methodology, including the simulation environment, reward function design, and ablation studies validating each reward component. Section~\ref{results} presents quantitative and qualitative comparisons of the proposed method against classical and learning-based baselines on simulation and our method on real hardware. Finally, Section~\ref{conclusion} summarizes the conclusions drawn from this work and outlines directions for future research. Our contributions are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item We design a coupled reward architecture, combining min-operator
pose coupling, asymmetric progress shaping, and an exponential \textcolor{black}{joint velocity}
penalty that resolves simultaneous position-orientation convergence
for the kinematic coupling inherent to non-spherical wrist manipulators,
without manual inter-objective weighting or staged training curricula.
This reward formulation, rather than the choice of underlying optimizer,
constitutes the core engineering contribution of this work. \textcolor{black}{The\linelabel{ln:scope_s} architecture is geometry-agnostic and broadly applicable. We focus on non-spherical wrists because their inherent kinematic coupling prevents analytical decoupling. Unlike spherical wrists, which admit closed-form solutions, non-spherical configurations require the reward formulation to explicitly enforce simultaneous convergence.\linelabel{ln:scope_e}}
%%%%%%%%%%%

%%%%%%%%%%%
\item \textcolor{black}{We show, through comparisons against alternative on-policy and off-policy algorithms (TRPO, SAC) trained on the identical dense reward, and against PPO trained on a sparse reward, that the proposed reward architecture is necessary for the controller's accuracy-smoothness trade-off, and that, among the optimizers tested, Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is the most robust to the conflicting gradients it introduces, making it an empirically justified choice of optimizer.}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\item We demonstrate that a controller trained purely on static,
joint-limit-aware reachable-pose samples with no trajectory
supervision, dynamic model, or task-specific retraining generalizes
zero-shot to four continuous trajectory geometries (circle, square,
lemniscate, 3-D Lissajous) in both simulation and physical hardware,
offering a reactive, online alternative to precomputed offline IK
pipelines for applications where target poses cannot be fully enumerated
in advance.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{related work}
To address the general inverse kinematics problem, several classical methods like numerical, analytical, combination of both and heuristic frameworks have been used in literature \cite{ABDORSIERRA2022100597}. Iterative solvers like TRAC-IK \cite{tracik} are widely used for their system-agnostic ability to handle joint limits and multiple constraints. However, even with a previous joint-state seed and the Distance parameter for solution selection, TRAC-IK remains fundamentally trajectory-unaware, each waypoint is resolved as an independent optimization with no inter-waypoint coupling, leading to configuration-branch switching and joint-space discontinuities during continuous trajectory tracking. \textcolor{black}{Practical\linelabel{ln:rw_mitig_s} implementations mitigate this behavior in three ways, each of which carries a trade-off rather than removing the underlying problem. First, seeding every solve with the previous solution keeps consecutive solutions on the same branch while the competing branches remain well separated; on a fully coupled 6-DOF problem this cannot be guaranteed along an arbitrary path, since branches approach one another near singular configurations and a local solver may converge to a different branch, or fail to converge, from its seed~\cite{tracik, dai2019global}. Consistent with this, the seeded TRAC-IK baseline evaluated in Section~\ref{sim_result} attains the lowest position error of any method on the planar curves, yet has the largest smoothness index of any method and degrades markedly on the 3-D Lissajous trajectory. Second, limiting the commanded joint velocity bounds the magnitude of any single discontinuity but does not prevent the branch change itself: clipping joint velocities individually distorts the path, whereas uniformly time-scaling the motion preserves the geometric path at the cost of a tracking lag that grows with the reference speed~\cite{faroni2018predictive}. Third, trajectory smoothing and lookahead filtering are effective for pre-planned motion profiles whose full path is known in advance; for targets determined online, only causal smoothing or online trajectory generation is available~\cite{kroger2010line, macfarlane2003jerk}, which bounds velocity, acceleration, and jerk but likewise smooths a branch change rather than preventing it, and adds a delay relative to the reference. The statements made in this work therefore concern the behavior that remains after these mitigations are applied, rather than numerical IK in an unmitigated form.\linelabel{ln:rw_mitig_e}}

Velocity-level controllers address this by operating directly in the differential domain. Resolved-rate motion control (RRMC) \cite{whiteney1969resolved} computes joint velocities using the Jacobian pseudo-inverse $(\dot{\theta}=J^{\dagger}\cdot\dot{x})$. While damped least squares(DLS)\cite{DLS, chiaverini1994review} introduce a damping factor $\lambda$ to prevent velocity singularities $(\dot{\theta} = J^\top(JJ^\top + \lambda^2 I)^{-1}\dot{x})$. Quadratic velocity-level  programming formulations \cite{velocityqp} further impose joint limit constraints explicitly, offering safer hardware deployment \cite{velocityqp_2}. These methods require an explicit, accurate Jacobian at every timestep. \textcolor{black}{On\linelabel{ln:rw_jacobian_s} non-spherical wrist manipulators, two further difficulties arise. First, position and orientation errors are coupled through all six rows of the Jacobian. Consequently, balancing translational and rotational accuracy requires complex, geometry-specific task-weighting matrices or damping schemes, and a setting tuned for one geometry need not transfer to another. Second, kinematic singularities no longer separate into distinct arm and wrist singularities, which makes the selection of a damping factor that is both safe near singular configurations and accurate away from them more difficult.\linelabel{ln:rw_jacobian_e}}

To improve upon the accuracy limitations of classical velocity controllers, hybrid approaches have emerged combining numerical methods with learned components \cite{wang2023two}. Learning based methods has been explored as a residual corrector atop numerical solvers \cite{jia2019finite,chen2019neural}, compensating for solver inaccuracies. An actor-critic RL approach was introduced as a correction signal in joint velocity space to the augmented PD controller for trajectory tracking on a 6-DOF industrial manipulator \cite{pane2016actor}. A gradient-free, model-free Bayesian Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) RL policy search utilized to circular trajectory control on 2-DOF manipulator in simulation to optimize the PD controller \cite{aghaei2022real}. A Soft Actor Critic(SAC)-based outer-loop stochastic reference correction policy learned directly from the 7-DOF Baxter manipulator. Their approach augments an existing vendor provided hardware controller with bounded corrective actions as an inheritance rather than learning from the environment \cite{pavlichenko2022real}. Further, \cite{zhang2019trajectory_1} apply distributed PPO with LSTM and GAE to trajectory tracking on a 3-DOF SCARA robot and mobile robot, using random reference state initialization and an exponential position-error reward.

Further, a compound controller combining a Sliding Mode Controller (SMC) with SAC-based RL augmented by Lyapunov stability constraints is proposed for the trajectory tracking of manipulators \cite{liu2022reinforcement, mao2025real}. While their method requires a complete dynamic model of the system, it demonstrates that model-based initialization significantly improves RL sample efficiency. A hybrid framework combining Twin Delayed Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (TD3) with an RRT-generated reference trajectory is proposed \cite{ota}. In this integrated planning and RL system, the reward function directly penalizes deviations from the RRT-based reference, constraining the agent to refine the pre-computed joint-space path. Further, \cite{V2025104178} formulate a collaborative PPO variant for tracking and vibration suppression of a flexible-joint manipulator, reporting that PPO-based policies converge faster and more stably than actor-critic and policy-gradient baselines under hardware-in-the-loop validation, a finding consistent with the convergence and
smoothness advantages of clipped on-policy optimization observed in the present work. In a similar way, \cite{li2021general} proposed a traditional path planner-DRL framework, combining RRT* with the Tsallis actor-critic algorithm to find optimized-energy IK solutions for redundant manipulators. This approach treats IK as a pose-reaching problem, solved point-by-point using an ANN-based solver.

Several DDPG-based methods have been explored to enhance trajectory tracking, these often depend on simplified assumptions or known dynamics. \cite{majumder2024enhancing} proposed a sequential DDPG approach for n-link manipulators, utilizing dual actor-critic networks trained progressively from 2D to 3D space to accelerate convergence. Similarly, \cite{zhang2019trajectory} implemented a distributed DDPG framework with prioritized experience replay, where multiple synchronous workers explore diverse policies to update a shared global network. Furthermore, \cite{hazem2025reinforcement} introduced hybrid variants, such as LC-DDPG and TD3-ADX, that learn joint torque commands by embedding an explicitly derived Euler-Lagrange dynamic model within the simulation. While these methods demonstrate efficacy in simulation, they frequently operate in joint torque space requiring precise manipulator dynamics, and lack real-time hardware validation, leaving the sim-to-real gap unaddressed. Moreover, these frameworks often overlook orientation control and motion smoothness, which are critical for scaling to 6-DOF manipulators.

% \textcolor{black}{The\linelabel{ln:rw_family_s} learning-based approaches above differ in what is learned, and this distinction is central to the present work. One family learns or optimizes the motion path itself: the output is a trajectory or reference, optimized offline or at the planning level for a task objective, which a separate controller then executes. Examples include the RRT-guided TD3 framework of \cite{ota} and the RRT*-based framework of \cite{li2021general}, as well as the reinforcement-learning path generation of \cite{khan2025reinforcement}, which generates energy-optimized, musculoskeletally informed movement paths for an ankle rehabilitation robot. The other family, to which the proposed method belongs, is reactive differential IK: the reference trajectory is supplied externally and need not be known in advance, and the controller maps the instantaneous pose error and robot state to joint-velocity commands at every control step. The two are complementary rather than competing; a learned or optimized path generator could supply the reference that a reactive differential IK controller tracks.\linelabel{ln:rw_family_e}}

\textcolor{black}{The\linelabel{ln:rw_family_s} learning-based approaches discussed above fall into two distinct families based on their output. The first family optimizes the motion path itself, generating an offline trajectory executed by a separate controller. Examples include RRT-guided frameworks \cite{ota, li2021general} and reinforcement-learning-driven path generation for musculoskeletal energy optimization \cite{khan2025reinforcement}. Despite these advances trajectory tracking, specifically on non-spherical wrist manipulators is unaddressed, IK solution introduces kinematic coupling that complicates both analytical solvers and standard RL reward formulations. Existing approaches either operate in torque space requiring explicit dynamic models, augment pre-existing classical controllers rather than learning from scratch. Furthermore, no prior work demonstrates that a policy trained purely on static pose reaching over a joint limit aware reachability dataset, without any trajectory supervision can generalize to continuous trajectory tracking at deployment. The proposed framework addresses this gap directly: a compact
256$\times$256 actor-critic architecture, trained end-to-end on this
coupled reward, learns a generalizable velocity-level mapping that
\textcolor{black}{extends\linelabel{ln:scopeclaim_s} to the continuous trajectories evaluated here without requiring}
trajectory demonstrations, dynamic models, or task-specific re-training\linelabel{ln:scopeclaim_e}. \textcolor{black}{The\linelabel{ln:rw_gap_s} proposed method is therefore a reactive differential IK controller rather than a trajectory generator. It resolves joint velocities online for externally supplied reference paths, circumventing the need for an explicit Jacobian or the analytical position--orientation decoupling absent in non-spherical wrists.\linelabel{ln:rw_gap_e}}

 \section{Methodology}
\label{method}
The proposed framework, illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:overallmethodology}, describes our methodology for training the trajectory tracking task. The observation space $\mathbf{s}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{19}$ provides the essential information required for the update of the policy. The action space $\mathbf{a}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{6}$ consists of six joint velocities sampled from the policy $\pi_\theta(\cdot|\mathbf{s}_\text{t})$. The training environment, implemented in NVIDIA Isaac Lab, utilizes 4096 parallel environments to train actor-critic neural networks with a $256\times256$ architecture. Further this setup uses Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) to give efficient joint velocities for the efficient trajectory tracking explained below in further sections.

\subsection{Simulation Environment}
\label{simulation}
Training is conducted in NVIDIA Isaac Lab with a physics timestep of $\Delta t$ = 1/120~s and a control decimation of 2, resulting in a policy frequency of 60~Hz. The Kinova Gen3 Lite robot is modeled as a USD asset with self-collisions enabled, ensuring physically realistic interactions during training. The joints are velocity-controlled, enabling direct command of joint velocities from the policy output. \textcolor{black}{The\linelabel{ln:calib_s} joint drive stiffness and damping parameters of the USD articulation were calibrated against the physical robot: constant joint-velocity commands were applied to the joints in simulation and on the Kinova Gen3 Lite, the resulting end-effector trajectories were recorded and compared, and the drive parameters were tuned until the simulated and measured trajectories matched. This calibration aligns the nominal actuator response of the simulation with that of the hardware.\linelabel{ln:calib_e}} Each episode spans 600~steps, with the robot initialized to its home configuration(zero position) at every reset. \textcolor{black}{Episodes\linelabel{ln:term_s} terminate only on time-out; no early termination on success, joint-limit contact, or collision is applied, so the policy is exposed to the full episode, including holding the pose after convergence. The reward contains no explicit joint-limit term: joint position limits are enforced by the PhysX articulation solver from the limits defined in the robot model, and joint-limit awareness enters training through the reachable-pose dataset, which is restricted to configurations within the soft joint limits (Section~\ref{subsec:dataset}).\linelabel{ln:term_e}} \textcolor{black}{At every episode reset, a target pose is drawn uniformly at random from the reachable-pose dataset described in Section~\ref{subsec:dataset}, and the corresponding pose error ($\Delta\mathbf{p}_t$, $\Delta\mathbf{q}_t$ in Table~\ref{tab:obs_action}) is computed relative to the robot's current end-effector pose.} Stochasticity
during training is introduced not by perturbing the target pose set itself,
but at the policy output, via the additive Gaussian action noise of
Eq.~\eqref{noise} (Section~\ref{rollout}), \textcolor{black}{which improves robustness to perturbations of the commanded joint velocities (Section~\ref{sim2real}).} The training is conducted on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5060 Ti (16~GB VRAM) with the hyper parameters used are listed in Table~\ref{tab:ppo_hyperparams}.

\subsection{\textcolor{black}{Reachable-Pose Dataset Generation}}
\label{subsec:dataset}
\textcolor{black}{
The\linelabel{ln:dataset_s} target poses used for training are drawn from a reachable-pose dataset generated once, offline, in NVIDIA Isaac Lab by exhaustive grid enumeration of the joint space (Fig.~\ref{fig:dataset_flow}). Each of the six arm joints is discretized into $N_s = 12$ uniformly spaced values over its soft position limits $[\theta_i^{\min}, \theta_i^{\max}]$, a resolution of $\Delta\theta_i = (\theta_i^{\max} - \theta_i^{\min})/(N_s - 1) \approx 0.49$~rad (${\approx}28^{\circ}$). Their Cartesian product yields $N_s^{6} \approx 3~M$ candidate configurations, all joint-limit feasible by construction.}

\textcolor{black}{The candidates are evaluated in batches of 4096 parallel environments. In each environment, the robot is reset to its home configuration and driven to the candidate configuration by joint position targets for 500 physics steps ($\Delta t = 1/60$~s), which is more than enough time to reach any candidate configuration, with the gripper held open. A contact sensor on every robot link monitors the net contact force throughout this motion, and a candidate is discarded if any link registers non-zero contact force at any step, i.e., self-collision or contact with the ground plane. For each retained configuration, the joint vector and the settled end-effector pose (position and quaternion) expressed in the robot base frame are stored. This yields approximately 1.5~M collision-free reachable poses (${\approx}50\%$ of the candidates), which are reused unchanged throughout the $5\times10^{8}$-step training run.} \textcolor{black}{Because targets are sampled on a joint-space grid, their Cartesian density is not uniform across the workspace. All evaluation trajectories are off-grid, so the tracking results in Section~\ref{results} reflect generalization between sampled targets rather than recall of them.\linelabel{ln:dataset_e}}

\subsection{Rollout Collection and Policy Update}
\label{rollout}
At each training iteration, experience tuples $\langle \mathbf{s}_\text{t}, \mathbf{a}_\text{t}, r_\text{t+1}, \mathbf{s}_\text{t+1}, \text{d}_{\text{t}} \rangle$ are collected across all parallel environments over $N_\text{steps}$ = 16 steps, yielding 65,536 transitions per rollout. The policy $\pi_\theta(\cdot|\mathbf{s}_\text{t})$ outputs a 6-dimensional joint velocity command $\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_\text{p} \in \mathbb{R}^6$, one per actuated joint of the Kinova Gen3 Lite. To improve robustness to real hardware actuator imprecision and to encourage exploration of the joint velocity space, additive Gaussian perturbation noise, $g \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.05^2)$ is injected at the environment interface before command execution as in Eq.~\eqref{noise}:  
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    \mathbf{a}_t &= \text{clip}\left(\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|\mathbf{s}_\text{t}) + g,\ -1,\ 1\right); \\ 
    & g \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 
    0.05^2\cdot\mathbf{I}_6)
\end{split}
\label{noise}
\end{equation}
This perturbation serves a dual purpose, prevents the policy from overfitting to the noiseless simulation dynamics and \textcolor{black}{improves\linelabel{ln:noise_s} robustness to actuator-level perturbations of the commanded joint velocities. It is not a substitute for full domain randomization: latency, backlash, payload variation, and actuator dynamics were not randomized in this work.\linelabel{ln:noise_e}} Advantage as in Eq.~\eqref{advantage}, estimates are computed via Generalized Advantage Estimation(GAE) using the critic value $V_\theta(\mathbf{s}_t)$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    \hat{A}_\text{t} &= \sum_{l=0}^{N_\text{steps}} (\gamma\lambda)^l \delta_{t+l}; \\
    & \delta_\text{t} = r_\text{t+1} + \gamma V_\theta(\mathbf{s}_\text{t+1}) - V_\theta(\mathbf{s}_\text{t})
\end{split}
\label{advantage}
\end{equation}
with discount factor $\gamma = 0.99$ and GAE parameter $\lambda = 0.95$. The actor $\pi_\theta$
is updated by maximizing the objective as in Eq.~\eqref{objective}:
\begin{equation}
L(\theta) = L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) - c_{1}\cdot L^{V}(\theta) + c_{2} \cdot\mathbb{H}(\pi_\theta(\cdot|\mathbf{s}_\text{t}))
\label{objective}
\end{equation}
where \[L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_\text{t}\left[\min\left(\rho_\text{t}(\theta)\hat{A}_\text{t},\ \text{clip}(\rho_\text{t}(\theta), 1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon)\hat{A}_\text{t}\right)\right]\]
the policy ratio $\rho_\text{t}(\theta) = \pi_{\theta}(a_\text{t}|s_\text{t})/\pi_{\theta_{old}}(a_\text{t}|s_\text{t})$ measures the deviation from the previous policy, the clipping range $\epsilon$ is 0.13, constrains the update step. The critic is updated by minimizing
mean squared error between the predicted value and the target return $
L^{V}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_\text{t}[(V_{\theta}(s_\text{t}) - \hat{R_\text{t}})^{2}]
$, and $\hat{R}_{t} = \hat{A}_{t} + V_{\theta}(s_{t})$ is the target return, value function coefficient $c_{1} = 0.5$, and entropy coefficient $c_{2}$ is 0.01. Both actor-critic networks are parameterized with Tanh activations and orthogonal initialization, updated jointly over 10 epochs per rollout via minibatch SGD with batch size 4096 as listed in Table~\ref{tab:ppo_hyperparams}.
\subsection{Reward Shaping}
\label{reward_function}
Designing a reward function for trajectory tracking on non-spherical wrist manipulators requires careful balance between position and orientation error convergence. The proposed reward function simultaneously minimizes both, while enforcing progressive convergence and smooth joint motion. The proposed reward function consists of three main components. \textcolor{black}{The\linelabel{ln:coupling_req_s} structure of this reward is dictated by the kinematics of the wrist. In spherical-wrist manipulators, disjoint joint groups decouple position and orientation, making additive rewards adequate. Conversely, offset wrists couple these objectives: every wrist joint simultaneously alters both position and orientation \cite{li2021novel}. This coupling imposes three requirements on the reward formulation. First, additive rewards are exploitable. A policy can maximize returns by converging the better-conditioned error channel while neglecting the other. The min operator (Eq.~\eqref{final_pose_reward}) removes this incentive by basing the reward strictly on the larger normalized error, forcing simultaneous convergence. Second, fixed reward weights fail to generalize because error sensitivity varies across the workspace. Normalizing and min-coupling the errors eliminates manual weighting entirely. Third, kinematic coupling introduces multiple solution branches. Shifting between branches causes large joint displacements without reducing pose error. This is penalized by combining the asymmetric progress term (Eq.~\eqref{progress_reward}) with the joint velocity penalty (Eq.~\eqref{energy_penalty}). The ablation study (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}) supports this account, although it does not isolate each requirement: the conventionally weighted variant \textcolor{black}{fails on both channels, and the min-coupled variants converge on both channels once the joint velocity penalty is included (Table~\ref{tab:ablation})}.\linelabel{ln:coupling_req_e}}

%%%%%%%%%%

%%%%%%%%%%%

\noindent \textbf{Pose Reward:}
The position and orientation errors are first normalized to a common scale:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
    {\mathbf{\text{e}}}_{\text{p}} &= \frac{\|\mathbf{\text{p}}_\text{target} - \mathbf{\text{p}}_\text{ee}\|_{2}}{\text{d}_\text{max}}, \\ {\text{e}}_{\text{o}} &= \frac{2\cdot \cos^{-1}(|\text{Re}(\mathbf{\text{q}}_\text{ee} \otimes \mathbf{\text{q}}_\text{target}^{*})|)}{\pi}
    \end{split}
    \label{pose_errors}
\end{equation}
where $\text{d}_{\text{max}}$ is the diagonal of the workspace, normalizing both errors ensures that the position and orientation errors are comparable in magnitude, preventing one from dominating the other. \textcolor{black}{All\linelabel{ln:quat_s} quaternions use the scalar-first convention $[w, x, y, z]$ and are expressed in the robot base frame. The relative orientation $\Delta\mathbf{q}_t = \mathbf{q}_\text{ee} \otimes \mathbf{q}_\text{target}^{*}$ (Hamilton product) is provided directly to the policy as the orientation-error observation (Table~\ref{tab:obs_action}). Since $\mathbf{q}$ and $-\mathbf{q}$ represent the same rotation, the orientation error $\text{e}_\text{o}$ is computed from the absolute value of the scalar part of $\Delta\mathbf{q}_t$, which makes it invariant to the quaternion sign and bounds the rotation angle to $[0, \pi]$, so that $\text{e}_\text{o} \in [0, 1]$. All reported orientation metrics use the same sign-invariant angle, $2\cos^{-1}(|\text{Re}(\Delta\mathbf{q}_t)|)$, in radians, i.e., before normalization by $\pi$.\linelabel{ln:quat_e}}

The errors $\in\mathbb{R}$ as in Eq.~\eqref{pose_errors}, then shaped through a combined inverse-quadratic and logarithmic term as in Eq.~\eqref{position_error_reward}:
\begin{equation}
    r({\mathbf{\text{x}}}) = \frac{2.5}{1 + 100 \cdot \mathbf{\text{x}^{\text{2}}}}  - 0.1\log(\mathbf{\text{x}} + 10^{-8})
    \label{position_error_reward}
\end{equation}
where $\text{x}$ is $\text{e}_\text{p/o}$.

\noindent The inverse-quadratic and logarithmic nature of the reward function helps to provide strong attraction in near-goal situations, and maintain a non-zero gradient far from the goal, avoiding the problem of sparse rewards.
The two terms are then coupled via a minimum operator as in Eq.~\eqref{final_pose_reward}:
\begin{equation}
    r_\text{pose} = \min(r({\mathbf{\text{e}}}_\text{p}),\ r({\mathbf{\text{e}}}_\text{o}))
    \label{final_pose_reward}
\end{equation}
The unconstrained algebraic combination of position and orientation rewards is susceptible to reward exploitation, where the agent converges to a suboptimal policy by maximizing one objective while leaving the other unconstrained. The pose reward as shown in Eq.~\eqref{final_pose_reward} applies the $\min$ operator to maintain simultaneous competency in both position and orientation throughout training. 

\noindent \textbf{Progress Reward:}
 Although the coupled pose reward shapes the magnitude of errors, it does not give an explicit incentive for the policy to reduce errors at every timestep. A policy could sustain a moderate error indefinitely and still receive a non-trivial reward. To address this, we introduce a progress reward as in Eq.~\eqref{progress_reward}, that explicitly penalizes stagnation and rewards per-step error reduction.
 At each timestep, the per-step error delta $\in\mathbb{R}$ as in Eq.~\eqref{change_in_error}, is computed for both position and orientation:
 \begin{equation}
    \Delta{\mathbf{\text{e}}}_\text{p} = {\mathbf{\text{e}}}_\text{p,t} - {\mathbf{\text{e}}}_\text{p,t-1}, \quad \Delta{\mathbf{\text{e}}}_\text{o} = {\mathbf{\text{e}}}_\text{o,t} - {\mathbf{\text{e}}}_\text{o,t-1}
    \label{change_in_error}
\end{equation}
where a negative delta indicates an improvement. Rather than applying progress rewards to both dimensions simultaneously, we apply it only to the dominant error, the dimension that is less in progress using the condition as in Eq.~\eqref{progress_cond}:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta{\mathbf{\text{e}}} = \begin{cases} \Delta{\mathbf{\text{e}}}_\text{p} & \text{if } \Delta{\mathbf{\text{e}}}_\text{p} \geq \Delta{\mathbf{\text{e}}}_\text{o}\\ 
    \Delta{\mathbf{\text{e}}}_\text{o} & \text{if } \Delta{\mathbf{\text{e}}}_\text{p} < \Delta{\mathbf{\text{e}}}_\text{o}
    \end{cases}
    \label{progress_cond}
\end{equation}
The progress reward is then shaped asymmetrically to ensure a continuous gradient through zero:
\begin{equation}
    r_\text{prog}(\Delta{\mathbf{\text{e}}}) = \begin{cases} 4(-\Delta{\mathbf{\text{e}}} + 0.25)^{0.5} - 2 & \Delta{\mathbf{\text{e}}} < 0 
    \\ -2\exp(2\,\Delta{\mathbf{\text{e}}}) + 2 & \Delta{\mathbf{\text{e}}} \geq 0
    \end{cases}
    \label{progress_reward}
\end{equation}
\noindent by offsetting the equations in Eq.~\eqref{progress_reward}, we prevent discontinuous reward cliffs that destabilize the value estimation. Square-root shaping on improvement provides diminishing returns for large progress steps, retarding aggressive overshooting. In contrast, the exponential penalty for regression imposes a sharply increasing penalty for error growth, creating a strong pressure on monotonic convergence.

\noindent \textbf{\textcolor{black}{Joint Velocity Penalty}:}
Trajectory tracking demands not only accurate end-effector pose reaching, but also smooth, continuous joint movements throughout the trajectory tracking. To enforce smooth motion throughout the entire trajectory, we introduce a continuous \textcolor{black}{joint velocity penalty on the squared} joint velocity commands at every timestep as in Eq.~\eqref{energy_penalty}:
\begin{equation}
\textcolor{black}{r_\text{vel}} = 1 - \exp\left(0.2\sum_{i=1}^{6}\dot{\theta}_i^2\right)
\label{energy_penalty}
\end{equation}
the exponential formulation imposes a sharply increasing cost as the aggregate joint velocity increases. \textcolor{black}{This\linelabel{ln:velpen_s} term acts as a control-effort (action) regularization on the commanded joint velocities; unlike energy-based formulations that model physical energy exchange, such as transactive energy in physical human--robot interaction~\cite{khan2024transformer}, it does not represent mechanical energy or power consumption.\linelabel{ln:velpen_e}}

\noindent The total reward is summation of pose, progression, and \textcolor{black}{joint velocity penalty} components as in Eq.~\eqref{total_sum}.
\begin{equation}
r_\text{t+1} = r_\text{pose} + r_\text{prog} + \textcolor{black}{r_\text{vel}}
\label{total_sum}
\end{equation}

\noindent converting from normalized radians to degrees per second as required by the hardware interface. Observation parity between simulation and hardware is maintained directly from actuator feedback through the Kinova Kortex API, preserving the exact observation structure $\mathbf{s}_\text{t} \in \mathbb{R}^{19}$ seen during training. 

The policy is deployed on an Intel NUC9 Extreme (16-core CPU, 32~GB RAM), achieving inference at $\approx$1.3~kHz. Since feedback reading and joint speed command dispatch are executed sequentially, the effective closed-loop control frequency is 20~Hz, well within real-time control requirements.

This 20~Hz effective control frequency is approximately one-third of the
60~Hz frequency used during training. Since the policy is a memoryless
mapping from instantaneous observation to velocity command with no
recurrent state, a lower deployment frequency only extends the
zero-order-hold (ZOH) duration of each command, it does not invalidate
any individual policy input. The per-step Gaussian action noise (Eq.~\eqref{noise}) and \textcolor{black}{joint velocity penalty} (Eq.~\eqref{energy_penalty}) used during training jointly bound output velocity magnitude, limiting the inter-sample integration error introduced by a longer hold. Consistent with this, no frequency-related degradation was observed: hardware position RMSE on the circular trajectory (6.3~mm) was lower than simulation RMSE (9.95~mm) at the tested trajectory speed ($0.1$~rad/s), \textcolor{black}{possibly aided by actuator damping and the low-level joint velocity controller smoothing the discretized commands, although this was not tested in isolation.} This should be read as evidence against degradation at the tested speed, not as general frequency invariance; robustness at substantially higher tracking speeds, where pose error drifts more within a single 50~ms interval, remains untested and is left to future work.

% \noindent\textcolor{black}{The\linelabel{ln:moving_s} policy receives neither the desired Cartesian velocity of the reference nor any history of past observations; it is a reactive state-feedback law acting on the instantaneous pose error and does not explicitly anticipate target motion. Trajectory tracking therefore proceeds as a sequence of short-horizon pose-reaching problems: at every control step the reference is resampled and the policy drives the current error toward zero. For a reference moving at Cartesian speed $v$, error-driven feedback of this kind, like classical resolved-rate control without a feedforward term~\cite{whiteney1969resolved}, is expected to settle into a bounded tracking lag that scales approximately as $v/K$, where $K$ is the effective closed-loop gain. At the evaluated speed ($r\omega = 15$~mm/s), the reference advances by only $\approx$0.25~mm per step at 60~Hz and $\approx$0.75~mm per step at 20~Hz, which is small relative to the observed tracking error, so each step remains close to the static reaching problem the policy was trained on. The asymmetric progress reward (Eq.~\eqref{progress_reward}) additionally penalizes every step in which the error grows, which favors a high effective gain and hence a small lag. As trajectory speed increases, the lag is expected to grow with $v$ until the commanded joint velocities approach their bounds, beyond which bounded tracking can no longer be maintained. The growth of this lag with speed is evaluated over 0.05--0.3~rad/s in Section~\ref{sim_result} (Table~\ref{tab:speed}); characterizing the speed limit itself, and providing the reference velocity or a short preview of upcoming way-points in the observation to enable anticipatory tracking, are left to future work.\linelabel{ln:moving_e}}

Because this spatial delta is negligible, each timestep closely mirrors the static reaching environment utilized during training. Furthermore, the asymmetric progress reward (Eq.~\eqref{progress_reward}) strictly penalizes step-wise error growth, implicitly maximizing $K$ to minimize steady-state lag. As trajectory speed increases, this lag grows proportionally with $v$ until joint velocity commands saturate, at which point bounded tracking fails. We evaluate this lag scaling across 0.05--0.3~rad/s in Section~\ref{sim_result} (Table~\ref{tab:speed}). Characterizing the absolute speed limit and incorporating velocity feedforward or look-ahead horizons for anticipatory tracking remain as future work.\linelabel{ln:moving_e}}

Joint velocity commands are bounded by hardware velocity limits enforced at the actuator level and further soft-constrained by the exponential \textcolor{black}{joint velocity penalty} in Eq.~\eqref{energy_penalty}, which imposes a sharply increasing cost on aggregate joint speed. \textcolor{black}{The\linelabel{ln:clip_s} hard clipping of policy outputs to $[-1,1]$ before dispatch bounds the magnitude of every command at both the simulation and hardware interfaces. Clipping limits only command magnitude, however, and does not by itself guarantee safety near kinematic singularities, at joint limits, or under unexpected contact. In simulation, joint position limits are enforced by Isaac Lab's physics solver rather than by the policy.\linelabel{ln:clip_e}}

\subsection{Baseline Comparison for Trajectory Tracking}
\label{reward_ablation}
To learn smooth and stable trajectory tracking, we rigorously evaluated existing DRL methods. Fig.~\ref{fig:training_curves} illustrates their mean terminal reward progression through training steps, normalized with respective maximum possible values to ensure fair comparison between differing reward structures. \textcolor{black}{The\linelabel{ln:sparse_s} sparse variants (suffix -S) replace the pose and progress terms with a tolerance signal of $-1$ per channel while the position or orientation error exceeds 4~cm or 0.1~rad, respectively, and $0$ otherwise, plus a bonus of $+10$ when both are within tolerance; the joint velocity penalty (Eq.~\eqref{energy_penalty}) is retained. PPO-D was trained with three seeds (42, 100, and 200), which converge to the same plateau (Fig.~\ref{fig:seed_curves}); Fig.~\ref{fig:training_curves} shows seed 42.\linelabel{ln:sparse_e}}

The performance divergence between PPO and SAC highlights a critical trade-off between gradient stability and sample efficiency in trajectory tracking. While SAC is widely regarded as a strong baseline for continuous control, \textcolor{black}{PPO outperformed it in this task. One\linelabel{ln:clipexp_s} plausible explanation is PPO's clipped surrogate objective, which restricts the magnitude of policy updates and may implicitly suppress high-frequency velocity fluctuations under the dense reward, although this mechanism was not isolated by a dedicated ablation. \linelabel{ln:clipexp_e}} This is evident in Fig.~\ref{fig:training_curves}, PPO-D achieves monotonic improvement, consistent with it \textcolor{black}{tolerating} the conflicting gradients introduced by \textcolor{black}{joint velocity penalties} and progression rewards, converging to a smooth control law with a mean terminal reward of $\approx$ 0.93. Conversely, SAC-D substantially underperforms its sparse counterpart (SAC-S), \textcolor{black}{which\linelabel{ln:sacd_s} is consistent with---though not definitive proof of---an underlying conflict between the maximum-entropy objective, which encourages exploratory joint motion, and the joint velocity penalty, which incentivizes near-idle behavior to minimize control effort at the expense of task progress.\linelabel{ln:sacd_e}}

TRPO-D, despite sharing PPO's conservative update logic through an exact KL-divergence constraint, converges slower and plateaus at a notably lower terminal reward $\approx$ 0.71, suggesting that the computational overhead of enforcing tight trust region boundaries impedes timely policy refinement compared to PPO's more tractable clipping approximation under dense reward supervision. \subsection{Reward Component Ablation Study}

No single classical baseline is uniformly best, and the pattern of
failure for each is a direct consequence of its underlying mechanism
rather than a tuning artifact. {\color{black}Motion\linelabel{ln:smooth_s} smoothness is quantified by the smoothness index $S$ as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:smoothness}, defined as the root-mean-square joint angular acceleration computed from the joint velocities $\dot{\theta}_{i,k}$ logged at every control step of a trial:
\begin{equation}
S = \sqrt{\frac{1}{6(N-1)}\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^{6}\left(\frac{\dot{\theta}_{i,k+1}-\dot{\theta}_{i,k}}{t_{k+1}-t_k}\right)^{2}}\quad [\text{rad/s}^{2}],
\label{eq:smoothness}
\end{equation}
where $N$ is the number of logged steps and $i$ indexes the six joints. Lower $S$ indicates smoother joint motion.\linelabel{ln:smooth_e}}

TRAC-IK resolves each waypoint as an independent constrained optimization, \textcolor{black}{seeded with its previous solution}. In a noiseless
simulation this allows near-exact convergence at every individual pose,
producing the lowest position RMSE among all methods on the circle
(1.2~mm) and lemniscate (1.07~mm), Table~\ref{tab:error_metrics_sim}. However, because each solve is independent with no explicit velocity-domain coupling between consecutive timesteps, small differences in optimizer convergence translate directly into joint-space discontinuities frame-to-frame. \textcolor{black}{This is consistent with its smoothness index of 52--62, the largest of any method: about three times that of SAC-S, 19--370 times that of TRPO-D, and three to four orders of magnitude above DLS, Velocity QP, and PPO-D.} On the Lissajous trajectory, where curvature and orientation change, the nearest-seed assumption that keeps TRAC-IK well-behaved on planar curves breaks down, and the solver begins to favor configuration branches discontinuous with the previous solution, position RMSE degrades by roughly 30$\times$ (1.2~mm $\rightarrow$ 35.3~mm) relative to the circle.

DLS computes joint velocities from a damped pseudo-inverse Jacobian with
a fixed damping factor $\lambda = 0.05$ and a single, undifferentiated
gain applied to the combined position-orientation error vector. On the
circle, where orientation is held constant, this fixed gain structure
strongly favors orientation correction, since the magnitude of angular
correction required to track a fixed orientation is small and well within
the controller's effective bandwidth, yielding the best orientation RMSE
of any method (7.7$\times10^{-4}$~rad) at the cost of a comparatively
large 16~mm position error. On the lemniscate, where orientation must
vary continuously alongside position, the same fixed gain cannot
adaptively reallocate correction authority between the two error
channels, and orientation RMSE rises to 0.036~rad, worse than the
proposed method's 0.024~rad on the identical curve. DLS's strong circle
orientation result is therefore a constant-orientation artifact of its
fixed-gain structure, not a general advantage over learned methods.

Velocity QP \textcolor{black}{uses\linelabel{ln:qp_s} the same Jacobian and pose error as DLS but solves a regularized quadratic program with explicit joint-velocity bounds at every step (Table~\ref{tab:baselines}).} \textcolor{black}{It attains the best orientation RMSE of any method on the lemniscate and the 3-D Lissajous ($3.9\times10^{-3}$ and $8.6\times10^{-3}$~rad), but does not lead in position on any curve (11.0--35.3~mm), degrading on the Lissajous to a level comparable with TRAC-IK; this is consistent with the single fixed weighting of the combined pose error, shared with DLS, favouring the orientation channel.\linelabel{ln:qp_e}} 


TRPO shares PPO's coupled reward (Eq.~\eqref{final_pose_reward}, Eq.~\eqref{progress_reward}, Eq.~\eqref{energy_penalty}) and its on-policy,
trust-region-constrained update rule, but enforces an exact KL-divergence
constraint rather than PPO's clipped surrogate objective. The exact
constraint requires a more expensive constrained optimization step per
update; under an identical training budget (Table~\ref{tab:wallclock}), this leaves TRPO's
policy less converged than PPO's at the same total timestep count,
producing both higher residual position/orientation error (8.9~cm /
0.37~rad on the circle) and substantially worse smoothness on the circle
(2.92) than PPO (0.0055), since incomplete convergence leaves residual
policy stochasticity in the action distribution.

\textcolor{black}{SAC-S\linelabel{ln:sacexp_s} settles at steady-state errors of $\approx$2.9~cm and $\approx$0.051~rad across all three trajectories. These values lie within the 4~cm~/~0.1~rad tolerance of its sparse reward, inside which the pose component of the reward is constant, so the policy has no incentive to reduce the error further; this is consistent with an offset independent of trajectory geometry. Its elevated smoothness index (18.9--19.8) may reflect the maximum-entropy objective, which the retained joint velocity penalty does not fully suppress within the tolerance zone; this was not isolated by a dedicated ablation.\linelabel{ln:sacexp_e}}

PPO does not achieve the lowest position RMSE in simulation on every
curve; rather, it is the most consistent method across geometries, with
position RMSE confined to a 9.9--16.7~mm band \textcolor{black}{(seed 42; 10.9--15.2~mm as three-seed means)} across circle, lemniscate,
and Lissajous, while every classical baseline degrades sharply on at
least one curve (TRAC-IK by $\sim$30$\times$ on Lissajous; DLS's
orientation by $\sim$47$\times$ on lemniscate). \textcolor{black}{PPO\linelabel{ln:smoothclaim_s} achieves the lowest mean smoothness index across all evaluated trajectories. It improves upon DLS and Velocity QP by a factor of 1.7--3 on the circle and lemniscate, performs comparably to Velocity QP on the Lissajous curve, and outperforms TRPO-D, TRAC-IK, and SAC-S by one to four orders of magnitude.\linelabel{ln:smoothclaim_e}}

\textcolor{black}{This\linelabel{ln:claim_s} consistency is in line with the structure of the proposed reward, although reward and optimizer were not varied independently for every baseline.} The min-operator coupling in Eq.~\eqref{final_pose_reward} prevents the policy from trading orientation accuracy for position accuracy under the shifting demands of each curve, which is \textcolor{black}{consistent with} PPO not exhibiting the orientation collapse seen in DLS on the lemniscate, where position and orientation
requirements vary continuously rather than remaining fixed as on the
circle. The asymmetric progress shaping in \textcolor{black}{Eq.~\eqref{progress_reward}} penalizes stagnation at every timestep regardless of trajectory geometry, consistent with PPO avoiding the persistent \textcolor{black}{in-tolerance} offset exhibited by SAC\textcolor{black}{-S, which corresponds to sustained, under-penalized error under a sparser reward}. The exponential \textcolor{black}{joint velocity penalty} in Eq.~\eqref{energy_penalty} acts directly on joint velocity magnitude, which \textcolor{black}{plausibly underlies} PPO's smoothness advantage over every baseline, independent of trajectory shape. \textcolor{black}{Neither the classical baselines nor the evaluated SAC-S use this reward, which may explain why their performance is uneven across geometries; TRPO-D uses the same reward but, as noted below, converges to a lower training reward.} \textcolor{black}{The evidence supports the reward as necessary rather than sufficient: with the same optimizer, the sparse reward fails (PPO-S), and with the same reward and training budget, TRPO-D converges to a lower reward (Section~\ref{reward_ablation}); the SAC comparison additionally differs in reward and training budget. The result therefore reflects the proposed reward combined with a stable on-policy optimizer.\linelabel{ln:claim_e}}

The sim-to-hardware transition reveals which methods rely on assumptions
that do not survive contact with real actuator dynamics. TRAC-IK's
position RMSE degrades by roughly 29$\times$ on hardware relative to
simulation (1.2~mm $\rightarrow$ 36.2~mm), the largest gap of any method.
This is consistent with its lack of a feedback term linking consecutive solves: each waypoint is resolved independently from joint
encoder feedback with no mechanism to detect or correct accumulated
real-world drift, transmission backlash, or actuator lag between
solves, errors that do not exist in a noiseless simulation. DLS degrades
by a comparatively moderate $\sim$3$\times$ (16~mm $\rightarrow$ 49~mm),
consistent with its damping factor being calibrated against an idealized
simulated Jacobian that does not account for joint compliance and
backlash present on the physical Kinova Gen3 Lite; its orientation
advantage on the circle persists on hardware (2.1$\times10^{-3}$~rad,
still the best of any method), since the fixed-orientation condition that
produces this advantage in simulation is preserved on hardware. SAC\textcolor{black}{-S}
degrades by $\sim$1.5$\times$ (29~mm $\rightarrow$ 45~mm)\textcolor{black}{; its in-tolerance offset from simulation persists, and the additional error is attributed to unmodeled hardware effects}.

\textcolor{black}{On\linelabel{ln:hwsingle} the circle, the only trajectory run by every method on hardware, PPO is the only method whose position error did not increase from simulation to hardware}: its position RMSE is 6.3~mm on hardware against 9.95~mm in simulation. \textcolor{black}{We\linelabel{ln:s2r_s} attribute this transfer to several factors acting together rather than to any single mechanism: (i) the policy is a closed-loop reactive controller that re-reads the measured robot state at every step, so errors arising from residual model mismatch are corrected continuously rather than accumulated; (ii) the action and observation interfaces are identical in simulation and on hardware (joint-velocity commands to velocity-controlled actuators, with the same 19-D observation), and the simulated joint drives were calibrated against the physical robot (Section~\ref{simulation}); (iii) the additive Gaussian action noise used during training (Eq.~\eqref{noise}) makes the policy robust to perturbations of the commanded velocities; and (iv) the evaluated regime of low-speed, unloaded, non-contact tracking keeps the effects of latency, backlash, and payload small. The policy also tolerated a threefold reduction in control frequency from training (60~Hz) to hardware (20~Hz) without retraining, which provides partial evidence of robustness to timing mismatch. Because latency, backlash, payload variation, and actuator dynamics were not randomized, this result is established for the tested operating regime and should not be extrapolated beyond it without further robustness evaluation.\linelabel{ln:s2r_e}} Across all four hardware trajectories, PPO maintains position RMSE between 6.3~mm and 15.4~mm without retraining (Table~\ref{tab:error_metrics_real}), with the lemniscate and 3-D Lissajous curves, which present orientation variation and curvature not
explicitly represented in the static-pose training distribution,
showing only a modest increase in error. \textcolor{black}{This generalization is consistent with} training on a dense, joint-limit-aware sample of the
reachable workspace pose-error space (Section~\ref{subsec:dataset}) rather than on trajectory-specific demonstrations: since the policy's input is
instantaneous pose error rather than a trajectory index, any sufficiently
dense coverage of that error space at training time transfers to
trajectory geometries never seen during training.

Taken together, the simulation and hardware results \textcolor{black}{are\linelabel{ln:together} consistent with the proposed reward structure, in combination with a stable on-policy optimizer, underlying} the method's accuracy-smoothness balance in simulation and its resistance to sim-to-real \textcolor{black}{position-error} degradation on hardware \textcolor{black}{within the tested operating regime}. Each \textcolor{black}{baseline's} hardware result degrades by an amount \textcolor{black}{consistent with} a documented limitation of its control structure, absence of inter-waypoint feedback for TRAC-IK, fixed-gain miscalibration for DLS, persistent \textcolor{black}{tolerance-band} offset for SAC\textcolor{black}{-S}.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{conclusion}
This work presented a reactive, online differential inverse kinematics
controller for non-spherical wrist manipulator, in which a novel coupled
reward function, combining a min-operator pose reward (Eq.~\eqref{final_pose_reward}), asymmetric
progress shaping (Eq.~\eqref{progress_reward}), and an exponential \textcolor{black}{joint velocity penalty} (Eq.~\eqref{energy_penalty}),
constitutes the central contribution rather than the choice of reinforcement
learning algorithm. Operating on a compact 19-dimensional observation of
joint state and instantaneous pose error (Table~\ref{tab:obs_action}), the policy requires no explicit Jacobian inversion, no online IK solve, and no trajectory-specific supervision, learning purely from joint-limit-aware static pose-reaching samples (Section~\ref{subsec:dataset}) and generalizing zero-shot to circular, square, lemniscate, and 3-D Lissajous trajectory tracking at deployment.

The simulation and hardware results (Section~\ref{results}) show that no single classical baseline is uniformly competitive across the evaluated metrics: TRAC-IK achieves the lowest position RMSE in simulation on planar curves but is \textcolor{black}{less smooth than every other method, by up to four orders of magnitude,} and degrades most severely on hardware (Table~\ref{tab:error_metrics_real}); DLS attains the best orientation tracking on the circle, but this advantage is specific
to constant-orientation motion and is lost on the lemniscate; and SAC\textcolor{black}{-S settles at a persistent offset within the tolerance of its sparse reward}. The proposed method does not achieve the lowest simulation error on every individual curve, but it is the only method whose position RMSE remains within a narrow band across all three simulated geometries, whose \textcolor{black}{mean} smoothness is the best of any method on every curve, and \textcolor{black}{whose\linelabel{ln:concl_hw} hardware position error on the circle did not exceed its simulation error in the trials conducted, whereas that of every hardware baseline increased} (Section~\ref{real_time_deployment}). \textcolor{black}{These\linelabel{ln:concl_claim} properties are consistent with the proposed reward being necessary for the observed accuracy--smoothness--robustness trade-off, in combination with a stable on-policy optimizer, rather than attributable to the reward alone.}

The present framework has several limitations that bound the scope of
these claims. Hardware validation is restricted to a single manipulator,
the Kinova Gen3 Lite; generalization to other non-spherical wrist platforms
(such as KUKA, ABB, or UR variants with offset wrist axes) has not been
tested, and the cost of porting the policy to a different kinematic
structure is unknown. \textcolor{black}{The\linelabel{ln:limseed_s} TRPO-D and SAC-S baselines, along with ablation variants (a)--(c), were trained using a single seed. Furthermore, all physical experiments represent single-trial executions; consequently, hardware-level run-to-run variance remains uncharacterized.\linelabel{ln:limseed_e}} \textcolor{black}{The\linelabel{ln:limabl_s} trajectory speed evaluation is restricted to simulation using a single training seed. Furthermore, the effects of varying control frequencies and training action noise were not ablated; characterizing these parameters is left to future work.\linelabel{ln:limabl_e}} \textcolor{black}{The\linelabel{ln:scopecond_s} empirical evaluation is bounded by specific operational constraints: four trajectory geometries executed at a single spatial scale within a localized workspace region, originating from a fixed initial configuration. Reference speeds were evaluated between 0.05--0.3~rad/s in simulation and fixed at 0.1~rad/s on hardware. Generalization to arbitrary spatial scales, broader workspace regions, or randomized initial states remains untested.\linelabel{ln:scopecond_e}} Hardware baseline comparison was restricted to the circular trajectory for TRAC-IK, DLS, and SAC\textcolor{black}{-S}, while the proposed method was further validated on lemniscate, 3-D Lissajous, and square trajectories without retraining; a head-to-head hardware comparison on the geometrically more complex trajectories remains open. Controller behavior near kinematic singularities also remains uncharacterized: although the robot is initialized at the zero configuration at every episode reset, the policy has not been explicitly trained on singularity-proximal configurations, and its recovery behavior at kinematic boundaries is therefore implicit rather than verified. Finally, because the policy has no explicit dynamic model or closed-form stability guarantee, it does not offer the formal safety guarantees available to model-based controllers, a property that may matter for contact-rich or safety-critical deployment even though it is not required for the reactive, non-contact tracking tasks evaluated here. \textcolor{black}{Energy-based\linelabel{ln:limits_s} approaches such as passivity-based control, which monitor the energy exchanged with the environment to maintain stable and safe physical interaction~\cite{khan2025quantum}, offer such guarantees, whereas velocity clipping in the proposed method bounds only command magnitude and does not ensure safety at joint limits or under unexpected contact. In addition, latency, backlash, payload variation, and actuator dynamics were not randomized during training, so the sim-to-real result is established for low-speed, unloaded, non-contact tracking only.\linelabel{ln:limits_e}}

Future work will address these limitations directly. Singularity-proximal
configurations will be incorporated during rollout collection to enable
verified recovery behavior at kinematic boundaries. Multi-seed training will be extended to the TRPO-D and SAC-S baselines and the reward-ablation variants, and the hardware baseline comparison will be extended to the non-circular trajectories. \textcolor{black}{The\linelabel{ln:future_s} reference velocity or a short preview of upcoming waypoints will be added to the observation to enable anticipatory tracking, and the range of trajectory speeds over which the reactive policy maintains bounded error will be characterized.} \textcolor{black}{Domain randomization over latency, backlash, payload, and actuator parameters, together with an external safety layer such as a control-barrier-function or passivity-based safety filter~\cite{khan2025quantum}, will be investigated to extend transfer and safety guarantees beyond the tested regime.\linelabel{ln:future_e}} Validation on additional
non-spherical-wrist manipulators will be pursued to test whether the
consistency observed here is specific to the Kinova Gen3 Lite or a more
general property of the reward design. Finally, the framework will be
extended to dual-arm manipulation, where the learned differential IK
controller would serve as a per-arm low-level policy for coordinated
bi-manual control, a natural extension toward collaborative assembly
operations requiring simultaneous precise trajectory tracking under contact
constraints.
